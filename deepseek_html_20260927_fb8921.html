Trade Failure Diagnosis Model

Momentum Masters — Advanced Insight Layer

Core Purpose

This is where real edge is built.

Not by winning trades — but by understanding why trades fail.

A losing trade is not failure... An unanalyzed loss is failure.

First Principle

Every failed trade can be traced to ONE broken link in the trading chain.

The Diagnosis Framework

1. Stock Selection 2. Market Context 3. Setup Quality 4. Entry Timing 5. Position Sizing 6. Risk Management 7. Exit Execution 8. Psychology

You must identify where exactly the breakdown happened.

Step-by-Step Diagnosis

1. Stock Selection Failure

Symptoms:

  • Stock never shows strength
  • Lags market or sector
  • Weak relative strength

Root Cause: Bought a weak/average stock

2. Market Context Failure

Symptoms:

  • Breakouts fail repeatedly
  • Good stocks don't move

Root Cause: Market was weak or uncertain. You traded against trend.

Truth: You were fighting the environment.

3. Setup Quality Failure

Symptoms:

  • No clear base or pattern
  • Random entry without structure

Root Cause: Forced trade without proper setup

Truth: There was no real opportunity — only impatience.

4. Entry Timing Failure

Symptoms:

  • Immediate drawdown after entry
  • Bought too early or too late

Root Cause: Entered before breakout OR chased after move

Truth: Timing error, not idea error.

5. Position Sizing Failure

Symptoms:

  • Small mistake → large damage
  • Emotional stress during trade

Root Cause: Oversized position

Truth: Risk was miscalculated.

6. Risk Management Failure

Symptoms:

  • Small loss becomes big loss
  • No clear exit point

Root Cause: No predefined stop. Ignored exit discipline.

Truth: Loss was allowed to grow.

7. Exit Execution Failure

Symptoms:

  • Profit turned into loss
  • Sold too early or too late

Root Cause: No exit plan. Emotional decision-making.

Truth: You mishandled the outcome.

8. Psychology Failure (Most Common)

Symptoms:

  • Fear, greed, hesitation
  • Breaking your own rules

Root Cause: Emotional override of system

Truth: You knew what to do — but didn't do it.

Chapter 2 — Stock Selection

Finding stocks that can actually go up a lot

1. Core Message (Most Important)

Great stocks are not found by guessing — they are identified through strength, price action, and institutional demand.

The best traders focus on leading stocks showing real momentum, not cheap or weak ones.

2. Simple Explanation

This chapter answers a very important question: How do you find stocks that can go up a lot?

All traders give slightly different methods, but they agree on key things:

  • Don't buy stocks just because they are cheap
  • Focus on stocks that are already showing strength
  • Look for big buyers (institutions) entering the stock
  • Timing matters — buying the right stock at the wrong time still leads to losses

In simple terms: Strong stocks tend to get stronger. Weak stocks tend to stay weak.

3. Key Ideas / Concepts

  1. Relative Strength (RS) — Stocks that are outperforming the market are more likely to continue performing. → Strength attracts more buyers.
  2. Price Action is Critical — Price movement tells the real story. → Ignore noise; focus on what price is doing.
  3. Growth Matters (Not Just Momentum) — Some traders (like Ryan) insist the stock must have strong earnings, not just rising price.
  4. Volume = Institutional Activity — High volume indicates big players are buying. → This drives large price moves.
  5. Avoid Chasing Stocks — Buying after a big move increases risk and reduces reward.
  6. Liquidity Matters — You must be able to enter and exit easily. → Low volume stocks require smaller position sizes.
  7. Avoid Bottom Fishing — Do not try to buy falling stocks. → Most big winners come from strength, not weakness.
  8. High Price ≠ Expensive — Higher-priced stocks are often better quality. → Cheap stocks are risky and often fail.
  9. Leaders Lead First — Strong stocks move before sectors become obvious. → Focus on individual stock strength first.
  10. Volume Still Works (Even with Dark Pools) — Despite market changes, volume remains reliable. → It still reflects supply and demand.

4. Author's Thinking (Mental Model)

The traders operate on a clear belief:

"The market rewards strength, not weakness."

Their mental framework:

  • Strong stocks = institutional demand
  • Institutions drive big moves
  • Price + volume = truth
  • Fundamentals (earnings) support sustainability

They reject:

  • Buying cheap stocks
  • Bottom fishing
  • Chasing extended moves

5. Why This Matters

Most beginners:

  • Buy "cheap" stocks
  • Try to catch bottoms
  • Chase after big moves

This leads to losses.

This chapter fixes that by teaching: where real opportunities exist, and how to identify strong stocks early.

If stock selection is wrong → everything else fails.

Chapter 3 — Position Sizing

The bridge between good ideas and actual performance

1. Core Message (Most Important)

Your success is not just about picking the right stock — it's about how much you invest in each idea.

Position sizing is the bridge between good ideas and actual performance.

2. Simple Explanation

Even if you pick great stocks:

  • If you invest too little → gains don't matter
  • If you invest too much → one mistake can destroy you

So traders focus on: How big should each trade be?

Top traders:

  • Increase size when things are working
  • Reduce size when uncertain
  • Never risk too much on one trade

Simple idea: Don't treat every trade equally. Size should depend on confidence + risk.

3. Key Ideas / Concepts

  1. Not All Trades Are Equal — Some setups are stronger than others. → Bigger size for high-quality setups.
  2. Risk First, Not Profit — Position size is based on how much you can lose. → Control downside before thinking of upside.
  3. Start Small, Then Build — Many traders begin with smaller positions. → Increase only if trade works.
  4. Concentration vs Diversification — Some traders concentrate (few big positions), others diversify (many small positions). → Both can work if managed properly.
  5. Adjust Based on Market Conditions — Strong market → larger positions. Weak/uncertain market → smaller positions.
  6. Liquidity Matters — Position size must match stock volume. → You should be able to exit easily.
  7. Pyramiding (Adding to Winners) — Increase position only after stock moves in your favor. → Never average down.

4. Author's Thinking (Mental Model)

The traders think like risk managers first, traders second:

"How much can I lose?" is more important than "How much can I make?"

Their approach:

  • Protect capital first
  • Scale exposure based on confidence
  • Let winners grow, cut losers quickly

They avoid:

  • All-in bets
  • Equal allocation to all trades
  • Emotional sizing

5. Why This Matters

Many traders:

  • Focus only on stock selection
  • Ignore position sizing

This leads to:

  • Overexposure → big losses
  • Underexposure → missed opportunities

Even with a 50% win rate, correct sizing can make you profitable.

Medical Analogy (Dosage)

  • Too little dose → no effect
  • Too much dose → dangerous
  • Correct dosage = optimal outcome

Same in trading: right size = controlled risk + meaningful returns.

8. Common Misunderstanding

  • "More conviction = go all in" → Dangerous thinking
  • "All trades should have equal size" → Ignores risk differences
  • "Average down to reduce loss" → Increases risk, not reduces it
  • "Big positions = big profits" → Also means big losses

9. Chapter Summary (5–8 bullets)

  • Position sizing determines real profitability
  • Risk management comes before profit
  • Not all trades deserve equal capital
  • Start small, increase when right
  • Adjust size based on market conditions
  • Liquidity affects position size
  • Add to winners, not losers
  • Avoid overexposure

Chapter 4 — Technical Analysis

Reading supply and demand through price and volume

1. Core Message (Most Important)

Technical analysis is not about predicting the future — it is about reading supply and demand through price and volume.

Charts help you understand what is happening now, so you can act with clarity instead of emotion.

2. Simple Explanation

This chapter explains how traders use charts to decide when to buy and sell.

All four traders strongly agree on one thing:

  • Price and volume are the most reliable information available
  • Everything else (news, opinions, stories) can be misleading

In simple terms:

  • If price is going up with strong volume → buyers are in control
  • If price is falling with high volume → sellers are in control

Charts are just a visual representation of this battle.

3. Key Ideas / Concepts

  1. Price is Truth — Price reflects all available information. → Focus on what the stock is doing, not what people say.
  2. Volume Confirms Moves — Volume shows strength behind price movement. → Big moves need big participation.
  3. Chart Patterns Repeat — Patterns exist because human behavior repeats. → Fear and greed create similar structures over time.
  4. Breakouts & Bases — Stocks often consolidate, then break out of that base.
  5. Pullbacks Are Normal — Stocks don't go straight up. → Healthy pullbacks offer opportunities.
  6. Timing Matters — Even a great stock can fail if bought at the wrong time. → Entry point is critical.
  7. Noise vs Signal — Short-term price moves can be misleading. → Focus on meaningful patterns, not random fluctuations.

4. Author's Thinking (Mental Model)

The traders believe:

"Charts are not predictions — they are evidence of behavior."

Their mindset:

  • Market = continuous auction
  • Price = final decision of all participants
  • Volume = intensity of that decision

They trust: what the market is doing.

They distrust: opinions, news, predictions.

5. Why This Matters

Most traders:

  • Follow news
  • Listen to tips
  • React emotionally

This creates poor decisions.

Technical analysis solves this by:

  • Providing objective signals
  • Reducing emotional bias

Without chart understanding, timing becomes guesswork.

Traffic Analogy

Think of price like cars moving — speed (price) + number of cars (volume).

  • Speed ↑ and many cars → strong movement
  • Speed ↑ but few cars → weak, may stop

8. Common Misunderstanding

  • "Charts predict the future" → No, they show current behavior
  • "More indicators = better decisions" → Often creates confusion
  • "Short-term moves are important" → Many are just noise
  • "News drives price" → Often price moves before news

9. Chapter Summary (5–8 bullets)

  • Price reflects all information
  • Volume confirms strength of moves
  • Chart patterns repeat due to human behavior
  • Breakouts follow consolidation phases
  • Pullbacks are normal and useful
  • Timing entry is critical
  • Ignore noise, focus on meaningful signals
  • Charts provide objective decision-making

This chapter answers: When should you sell a stock?

All traders agree:

  • Selling is harder than buying
  • There is no single rule — context matters

But they follow clear principles:

  • Cut losses quickly (already covered)
  • Protect profits when stock shows weakness
  • Let strong stocks run when conditions are right

In simple terms, sell when:

  • You are wrong
  • The trend weakens
  • The opportunity is over

3. Key Ideas / Concepts

  1. Cut Losses Fast — If trade goes wrong → exit quickly.
  2. Take Partial Profits — Some traders sell part of position to lock gains. → Reduces pressure and risk.
  3. Let Winners Run — Big money comes from holding strong stocks. → Don't exit too early.
  4. Sell on Weakness — Signs of weakness: heavy selling (high volume down move), failed breakouts. → Indicates institutions may be exiting.
  5. Trailing Stops — As stock rises → adjust stop-loss upward. → Protect gains while allowing growth.
  6. Different Strategies Exist — Some traders sell quickly, others hold longer. → But all manage risk carefully.
  7. Emotional Control is Critical — Greed → holding too long. Fear → selling too early.

  • Profit protection second

  • Holding losers
  • Selling winners too early without reason

5. Why This Matters

Most traders fail because:

  • They sell winners too early
  • They hold losers too long
